## Part 1: What safety costs construction companies

### 1.1 The injury baseline

- **Fatalities.** In 2024, 1,034 people died working in the construction industry. That is more deaths than any other private industry: transportation and warehousing had 865 and agriculture 475. The construction fatality rate was 9.2 per 100,000 full-time-equivalent (FTE) workers [1]. Across all workers the rate was 3.3 [2].
- **The rate is flat.** Construction's fatality rate has stayed between 9.2 and 10.2 per 100,000 every year from 2012 to 2024 [21]. Construction safety has plateaued, not improved.
- **Falls dominate.** Falls, slips and trips caused 389 construction deaths in 2024, about 38% of the total [3]. By occupation, BLS counts 1,032 deaths among construction and extraction workers, 370 of them from falls, slips and trips [2]. (Industry counts and occupation counts are separate BLS groupings, so they don't add together.)
- **Nonfatal injuries, and underreporting.** On paper, construction's 2024 injury rate of 2.2 cases per 100 FTE workers is *lower* than private industry's 2.3 [21], even though construction's fatality rate is nearly three times higher. The same report estimates that private-industry injuries are really 5.0 to 7.5 million a year, against about 3.1 million reported [21]. OSHA's own analysis concludes that "many, and perhaps the majority, of work-related injuries are not recorded by employers" [15]. **This gap is the opening for InSite: companies are paying for injuries they aren't measuring.**

### 1.2 Workers' compensation insurance

Every state requires employers to carry workers' comp. Premiums are set by trade class, and construction trades sit near the top.

- **National scale.** Employers pay "more than $1 billion per week" in direct workers' comp costs (Liberty Mutual 2025 Workplace Safety Index, cited by OSHA) [5].
- **Construction-specific.** In Liberty Mutual's 2026 index (2023 data), falls to a lower level were construction's #1 cost driver at **$2.64 billion a year, 26.1% of the industry's costs**. Construction is the only one of the eight industries studied where falls rank first [4]. That share implies roughly **$10.1 billion a year** in serious, nonfatal injury costs for construction *(our calculation)*.
- **Per-hour labor overhead.** In June 2026, construction employers paid **$4.42 per hour worked** in legally required benefits, the BLS cost category that has included workers' comp. Private industry overall paid **$3.40**. That is 8.5% of construction compensation versus 7.2% overall, on total compensation of $51.96 per hour against $46.89 [8]. BLS will stop including workers' comp costs in this series starting with December 2026 data [9].
- **Price direction.** In California, the regulator approved an average claims-cost benchmark of **$1.65 per $100 of payroll** for policies from September 1, 2026 [10]. That figure is an average across all trade classes; the regulator directs the rating bureau to set each class's rate relative to it. Nationally, contractors' workers' comp renewals were "largely stable," with many renewing "flat to up 3%" in mid-2026 [11].

### 1.3 The hidden multiplier: indirect costs

The insurance claim is only part of what an injury costs. OSHA's *Safety Pays* estimator adds indirect costs on a sliding scale based on the direct (claim) cost [6]:

| Direct cost of claim | Indirect-cost multiplier |
|---|---|
| $0 to $2,999 | 4.5× |
| $3,000 to $4,999 | 1.6× |
| $5,000 to $9,999 | 1.2× |
| $10,000 or more | 1.1× |

The multipliers come from a Business Roundtable construction-safety publication built on a Stanford civil engineering study, so they are construction-derived. OSHA notes that "the less serious the injury the higher the ratio of indirect costs to direct costs" [6]. Indirect costs include supervisor time, incident investigation, paperwork, crew downtime, retraining and schedule slip.

*Example (our calculation):* a $2,500 minor claim carries about $11,250 in indirect costs at 4.5×, for a total near $13,750. Many small incidents are where the hidden cost piles up.

AFL-CIO applies a 2×–5× indirect multiplier to Liberty Mutual's direct figures and estimates that employers pay **$177 billion to $354 billion a year** in total direct and indirect costs for the most disabling injuries across all industries [21].

### 1.4 Liability insurance and litigation exposure

Workers' comp shields the direct employer from most employee lawsuits (see §2.2). General contractors, owners and subcontractors are still exposed to third-party suits, and they buy general liability (GL) and umbrella/excess coverage for that.

- **Mid-2026 construction renewals** (Gallagher, via Risk & Insurance) [11]:
  - Excess liability: **+5% to +30%**, with steeper increases for higher-hazard contractors.
  - General liability: low to mid-single-digit increases, steeper for street and road work and residential for-sale construction.
  - Workers' comp: flat to +3%.
  - Builder's risk and property: **decreasing**, down 5% to 20% or more for good risks.
  - The pattern: property is getting cheaper while casualty (injury-driven) coverage keeps getting more expensive.
- **Nuclear verdicts.** In 2025, companies faced nearly 200 jury verdicts of at least $10 million each, totaling more than $25.6 billion. That was up 40.7% from 2024 and included more than 40 verdicts above $100 million [12]. Gallagher links third-party litigation funding to social inflation and nuclear verdicts [11].
- **New York, the extreme case.** New York Labor Law §240 (the "Scaffold Law") puts absolute liability for elevation-related injuries on owners and general contractors. One broker told Business Insurance that insuring a high-rise costs about **3% to 3.5% of construction value in Pennsylvania versus "lucky if I can get it done for 9%" in New York**, with GL deductibles around $250,000 versus $2 million [14]. A 2014 Rockefeller Institute estimate put the law's insurance cost at $785 million a year, though worker-advocacy groups dispute that study [14].

### 1.5 Regulatory penalties

- **Maximum OSHA penalties** for penalties assessed after January 15, 2026 [7]:
  - Serious, other-than-serious, or posting violation: **$16,550** per violation.
  - Failure to abate: **$16,550** per day past the abatement date.
  - Willful or repeated: **$165,514** per violation.
- **What OSHA actually collects is much lower.** In FY2025, the average federal penalty for a serious violation was **$4,678** and the median federal penalty in a worker-death case was **$16,550** [21]. In New York State, the average OSHA fine in a construction fatality case fell to **$25,295** in 2024, the lowest since 2017 [20].
- **Takeaway:** fines are a rounding error next to insurance, indirect costs and litigation. Prevention pays off by avoiding claims and lawsuits, not by avoiding OSHA fines.

### 1.6 Legal and dispute costs

- **Contract disputes (all causes, not only safety).** Arcadis's 16th annual survey found the average U.S. construction dispute in 2025 was **$56.0 million** and took **12.2 months** to resolve. The largest reported dispute was $100 million, 57.9% of respondents handled more disputes in 2025 than in 2024, and 75% expect more in 2026 [13]. Safety incidents feed into these disputes through delay claims and stop-work orders.
- **Injury litigation** reaches GCs and owners through third-party claims and, in New York, the Scaffold Law (§1.4). Those costs show up as GL and excess premiums, high deductibles and defense spending [11], [14].

### 1.7 Prevention has measurable returns

OSHA's business-case page cites the following [5]:
- Cal/OSHA inspections were followed by a **9.4% drop in injury claims** and **26% average savings on workers' comp costs** (Levine, Toffel & Johnson, *Science*, 2012).
- Liberty Mutual's CFO survey: **each $1 invested in injury prevention returns $2 or more**.
- Sites in OSHA's Voluntary Protection Programs average a lost-time (DART) rate **52% below their industry average**.

## Part 2: What injuries cost workers, and how they are protected

### 2.1 Who actually pays for workplace injuries

OSHA's 2015 report *Adding Inequality to Injury* [15] found:
- Workers' comp pays **about 21%** of the lost wages and medical costs of work injuries and illnesses.
- Workers, their families and their private health insurance pay **nearly 63%**.
- Taxpayers pay the remaining **16%** through Medicare, Medicaid, SSDI and similar programs.
- Even with workers' comp benefits, injured workers' incomes are **almost $31,000 (15%) lower over the 10 years after an injury**, based on a New Mexico study. The figure excludes the many injured workers who never enter the system.
- **Fewer than 40%** of eligible injured workers apply for workers' comp at all.

A DOL Chief Evaluation Office summary of the same research puts it this way: counting lost earnings, lost fringe benefits and lost household production, injured workers and their families bear **half** of the estimated $250 billion total cost of injuries (2007 data) [16].

### 2.2 How workers' comp is structured (the "grand bargain")

- **No-fault in exchange for no lawsuit.** Workers get benefits without proving the employer was at fault. In return, under this "no-fault" system, "workers have lost the right to sue their employer" [15]. That means no damages for pain and suffering, and it is why injured workers who want full compensation look to *third parties* such as GCs, owners and equipment makers (§1.4).
- **State-run.** Private-sector workers are covered by their *state's* workers' comp system. DOL runs only federal programs (federal employees, longshore and harbor workers, black lung, energy employees) [17]. Benefits include wage replacement, medical treatment and vocational rehabilitation [17].
- **Wage replacement is partial and capped.** Using California as an example [18], [19]:
  - Temporary disability pays **two-thirds of gross (pre-tax) wages**, within minimum and maximum rates set by law, and these benefits are not taxable [18].
  - For 2026 the weekly benefit is capped at **$1,764.11** and floored at **$264.61** [19].
  - Payments start only when a doctor says the worker can't do their usual job for **more than three days**, or the worker is hospitalized overnight [18].
  - Most injuries are limited to **104 weeks** of temporary disability within five years of the injury date [18].
  - The worker must file a DWC 1 claim form. Missing the one-year deadline can forfeit benefits [18].
- *Example (our calculation):* using the June 2026 average construction wage-and-salary cost of $36.13 per hour worked [8], a 40-hour worker earns about $1,445 a week. Temporary disability would pay about $963 a week, a gross cut of about $482 a week, partly offset because the benefit isn't taxed [19]. And if the doctor's work restriction lasts three days or less with no overnight hospital stay, no temporary disability is paid at all [19].

### 2.3 Federal OSHA rights

| Protection | What the rule says | Ref |
|---|---|---|
| **Employer pays for PPE** | Protective equipment "shall be provided by the employer at no cost to employees". Exceptions include ordinary safety-toe boots and prescription safety glasses worn off-site, everyday clothing, and weather gear. The employer must also pay for replacement PPE unless it was lost or intentionally damaged. | [25] |
| **Right to complain and request an inspection** | Workers can file a confidential safety complaint and request an OSHA inspection if they believe there is a serious hazard. | [26] |
| **Right to refuse dangerous work** | Protected only if *all* of these hold: the worker asked the employer to fix the danger where possible; refused in good faith; a reasonable person would see a real danger of death or serious injury; and there is no time to fix it through normal enforcement. | [27] |
| **Anti-retaliation (Section 11(c))** | Retaliation for safety complaints or injury reports is illegal. **A complaint must be filed within 30 days** of the retaliation. | [28] |
| **Access to injury records** | Employees and former employees can get the OSHA 300 Log by the end of the next business day, plus their own 301 incident report. Employers may not discriminate against employees for reporting injuries. | [29] |
| **Employer reporting deadlines** | Employers must report a fatality to OSHA within 8 hours, and an in-patient hospitalization, amputation or loss of an eye within 24 hours. | [30] |

**The enforcement gap.** These rights depend on enforcement that is thin [21]:
- **1,651** federal and state OSHA inspectors cover **12 million workplaces** and **155 million workers**. That is one inspector per 93,877 workers, compared with one per 54,952 in 1991.
- At current staffing, federal OSHA could inspect each workplace **once every 191 years**.
- Only **144** worker-death cases have been criminally prosecuted under the OSH Act since 1970.
- The tight **30-day** retaliation deadline [28], combined with fear of retaliation, is one reason so many injuries go unreported and unclaimed; OSHA lists fear of retaliation among the reasons workers don't file [15]. Workers rarely have their own record of what happened. This is the "insufficient evidence" problem in the pitch, and a worker-owned incident record from InSite addresses it directly.

### 2.4 Workers who fall through the cracks

- **Misclassification.** Employers who wrongly classify employees as independent contractors avoid workers' comp and unemployment insurance premiums, and the self-employed fall outside OSHA coverage [15]. In 2015 OSHA cited research estimating that **37.7% of Texas**, **35.2% of North Carolina** and **15.5% of Florida** construction workers were misclassified, more than 500,000 workers in those three states alone [15]. In 2026, EPI estimated that misclassification costs a construction worker **$13,186 to $20,399** in lost income and benefits [23], [24].
- **Latino and immigrant workers.** Hispanic or Latino workers had **1,229** fatal work injuries in 2024; **842** of them (68.5%) were foreign-born [2]. Of those 1,229, **451** were in construction and extraction occupations [21]. CPWR found Latino construction deaths **rose 107.1%** between 2011 and 2022 to 408, while non-Hispanic construction deaths rose 16.5%, from 583 to 679 [22]. In 2021–2022, Hispanic construction workers made up 34.5% of days-away-from-work injuries and 47.3% of job-transfer or restriction injuries [22].
- **Non-union sites.** Of New York State construction fatalities investigated by OSHA in 2024, **81%** of the workers who died were non-union [20].
- **Substance use.** CPWR reported that drug-overdose deaths in construction fell **28.8%**, from 135.0 to 94.8 per 100,000 workers, between 2023 and 2024 [21]. The same report notes that previously injured workers have higher rates of opioid poisoning [21].

## Part 3: The two sides side by side

| | Company | Worker |
|---|---|---|
| **Insurance** | Workers' comp premiums ($4.42/hr in legally required benefits vs. $3.40 economy-wide [8]); GL and excess premiums rising 5–30% [11] | Workers' comp replaces about 2/3 of wages, capped, after a waiting period [18], [19], and covers only ~21% of real costs [15] |
| **Indirect costs** | 1.1× to 4.5× the claim [6] | ~$31,000 in lost earnings over 10 years [15] |
| **Legal** | Third-party suits, nuclear verdicts ($25.6B in 2025 [12]), disputes averaging $56M [13] | Can't sue the employer [15]; 30-day retaliation deadline [28]; fewer than 40% even apply for benefits [15] |
| **Regulatory** | Up to $16,550 / $165,514 per violation [7], but a median of $16,550 for a death [21] | Rights exist on paper; an inspection comes about once every 191 years [21] |
| **Information gap** | Underreported injuries mean unmeasured costs [15], [21] | No independent record when a claim or dispute arises |
